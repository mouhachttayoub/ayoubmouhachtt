Marketing pre-mortem: find the reasons a launch could fail

Run a marketing pre-mortem to uncover launch risks, assign owners and define pause conditions with a practical failure-scenario worksheet.

Ayoub’s Growth Notebook3 min readPrepared 13 October 2026AI-assisted guide
The short answerA marketing pre-mortem asks the team to imagine a launch has failed and identify plausible reasons. Turn each reason into a prevention action, detection signal or recovery plan. The exercise is useful only when it changes the launch brief and gives someone responsibility.

Define failure before imagining it

Choose a concrete outcome and horizon. “The launch was disappointing” invites vague opinions. “The campaign spent its test budget but produced few qualified buyers, while support complaints increased” gives the group something specific to investigate.

Invite people who see different parts of the journey: marketing, product, fulfillment, sales and support. Let them write risks independently before discussion. A person who handles customer complaints may notice a problem the campaign owner does not see.

Sort risks by where they happen

Failure scenarioEarly warningPrevention or response
Offer overpromisesRepeated questions about unavailable featuresVerify claims against the actual product
Follow-up is slowQualified inquiries wait without an ownerAssign coverage and escalation
Tracking is incompleteKnown test purchases are missingValidate events before scaling
Inventory is insufficientAvailable stock falls below commitmentsRestrict promotion and communicate availability
Automation repeats itselfCustomers receive duplicate remindersTest exits, suppression and event handling

These are example categories, not a universal risk ranking. The highest priority depends on the likelihood, consequence and reversibility of the failure in your business.

Worked example: a campaign that sells the wrong promise

Illustrative example: an ad says an analytics service is ready immediately, but new customers need a manual data review. The launch team imagines a spike in disappointed buyers and refunds. The fix is not merely another disclaimer at the bottom of the page.

Rewrite the main promise, state the review step before payment and prepare a clear confirmation message. Give support a single owner for stalled setups. Monitor questions about timing as a guardrail. This changes the actual purchase experience rather than protecting an inaccurate headline with fine print.

Decide which risks block launch

Some issues can be observed safely during a limited test. Others invalidate the offer or measurement immediately. Mark risks as launch blockers, watch items or accepted limitations, and explain why. A lack of reliable stock information may block a sales push; uncertain creative preference may simply be what the test is intended to learn.

Record a pause condition in operational terms. “Pause if customers are upset” is hard to execute. “Pause this promise if the advertised setup cannot be delivered to new buyers” gives the team a direct decision. Keep a rollback path for the experience and its communications.

Run the meeting in a useful way

Choose one upcoming launch, write a specific failure outcome and collect independent reasons. Leave the meeting with five or fewer prioritized actions, each with an owner and readiness check. Revisit the sheet after launch to compare predicted risks with actual problems.

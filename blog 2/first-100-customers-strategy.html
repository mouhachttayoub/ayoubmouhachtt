Growth strategy

How to get your first 100 customers without pretending every channel scales

Plan your first 100 customers around a narrow segment, direct learning and verified customer outcomes rather than a broad launch or vanity sign-ups.

Ayoub’s Growth Notebook3 min readPrepared 13 October 2026AI-assisted guide
The short answerGetting your first 100 customers is a learning problem before it is a scaling problem. Start with a specific group you can serve, a clear paid offer and a repeatable way to speak with suitable buyers. Track completed customer outcomes, not just registrations or promises to buy.

Decide what counts as a customer

A free account, an unpaid order and a paying active customer are different milestones. Define the commitment you want to count and the useful outcome that follows it. For a store, you might count delivered paid orders; for software, paid accounts that reach a defined first outcome.

Choose a narrow buying situation. “Everyone who needs marketing” does not help you reach the right person. A recognizable problem, timing trigger and current workaround give you a reason for a conversation and a basis for evaluating fit.

Start with conversations you can learn from

Paul Graham's essay argues that early founders often need to recruit and help users personally. Treat that as a way to learn, not a prediction that any manual tactic will work. Use relevant introductions, communities where participation is permitted and direct conversations with people facing the problem.

Do not buy an indiscriminate contact list or pretend a mass message is personal research. Explain who you help and what you are asking. Keep refusal easy. A small set of suitable conversations can expose an offer problem more clearly than a large pile of irrelevant traffic.

Worked example: the target is not 100 sign-ups

Illustrative example: a small software team speaks with 40 suitable businesses. Twelve try a paid pilot, eight complete the intended workflow and five continue after the pilot. The useful review is why four pilots did not reach the outcome and why three activated accounts did not continue.

You cannot infer a reliable market-wide conversion rate from this small, selected group. You can identify specific questions for the next cohort: integration compatibility, pricing, implementation effort or whether the recurring task is valuable enough.

Make the next ten easier to serve

Record objections, onboarding questions and the work required to deliver. Turn repeated explanations into a demonstration or checklist. Automate only the steps you understand well enough to specify. A repeatable process should preserve the useful conversation rather than erase it.

Set a learning budget and review the cost of founder time. Manual acquisition is not free merely because it uses no ad spend. Once delivery is consistent and the customer pattern is clearer, choose a channel that can reach more people in that same situation.

Plan the first cohort

Write your customer definition, narrow segment and first useful outcome. Aim to learn from the next ten suitable buyers before designing a campaign for the next thousand. Review the concierge onboarding guide to capture what should eventually become self-service.

Primary-source context: Paul Graham: Do Things that Don’t Scale. Checked 2026-10-13.

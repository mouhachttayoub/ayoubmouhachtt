@@ customer-segmentation-profitability
META: Segment customers by contribution, acquisition cost and service effort with a worked example that separates revenue from customer profitability.
ANSWER: Profitability-based segmentation compares the money customers contribute with the cost of acquiring and serving them over the same window. High revenue is not automatically a valuable segment. Keep acquisition, variable delivery costs and support effort visible before choosing where to invest.
RELATED: first-order-vs-repeat-customer-profit,fully-loaded-customer-acquisition-cost,contribution-margin-waterfall

## Start with an actionable segment

Choose characteristics that the team can recognize and serve: buying situation, product need, order pattern or required support. A label such as “high-value customers” describes a result but does not explain how to find similar buyers. Keep segment definitions stable while comparing outcomes.

Avoid using future knowledge to assign a customer's original segment. If you label everyone who eventually renews as a “good-fit acquisition audience,” you have created a hindsight category. Record what was knowable at the time of purchase, then observe what happens afterward.

## Put costs on a consistent basis

Compare the same cohort age, currency and revenue definition. Subtract refunds and variable fulfillment costs before assessing contribution. State whether support is measured as actual cost, estimated hours or an allocated amount. Estimates can be useful, but they should not look more precise than the underlying record.

| Component | Question to resolve |
| Revenue | Is this collected revenue, booked revenue or an estimate? |
| Variable cost | Which delivery, payment and product costs increase with sales? |
| Acquisition | Is this media-only or fully loaded CAC? |
| Service | Does the segment require more setup or support? |
| Observation window | Are both segments equally mature? |

Fixed overhead is a separate consideration. A positive contribution segment still needs enough total contribution to help cover the business's operating costs.

## Worked example: lower revenue, stronger contribution

Illustrative example: over a comparable period, Segment A contributes $80 per customer before acquisition and costs $50 to acquire. Segment B contributes $110 before acquisition but costs $95 to acquire. After acquisition, A contributes $30 and B contributes $15. B's higher revenue would not by itself justify prioritizing it.

If B also requires $20 of additional onboarding effort, its contribution falls to negative $5 under these assumptions. Check whether the effort is temporary and whether later retention compensates for it. Do not insert an optimistic lifetime-value forecast simply to rescue the preferred segment.

## Choose a response, not just a ranking

A weak segment may need a different price, a more accurate promise, a narrower product fit or a simpler support model. Excluding it from all marketing is not the only available decision. A profitable segment can also become harder to acquire as reach expands.

Report ranges where costs are uncertain and test sensitivity. If changing the support estimate by a few dollars reverses the conclusion, collect better service data before a major budget move. Keep customer fairness and service obligations separate from the commercial ranking.

## Build your segmentation sheet

Take two recognizable customer groups and compare revenue, variable costs, acquisition and service effort at the same age. Write the operational action each result would justify. For the underlying cost structure, use the [contribution margin waterfall](contribution-margin-waterfall.html).

@@ competitive-alternative-map
META: Build a competitive positioning map around current workarounds, switching costs and buying criteria instead of copying a competitor feature grid.
ANSWER: Competitive positioning starts with the alternatives customers can choose, including spreadsheets, internal work, another supplier and doing nothing. Map the buying task, benefits, drawbacks and switching effort. A direct competitor list alone can miss the option that actually wins the decision.
RELATED: jobs-to-be-done-switch-interview,b2b-bottom-funnel-content,marketing-positioning-statement

## Ask what the customer would do instead

Use recent buying conversations to identify alternatives actually considered. Avoid assuming the largest brand in the category is the main rival. A founder may choose to keep a manual process because migration seems riskier than the inconvenience.

Separate direct evidence from your interpretation. “Customer uses spreadsheets today” is observed. “Customer wants a centralized dashboard” needs supporting evidence. An alternative map becomes useful when it explains the tradeoff the buyer recognizes, not when it lists features your product happens to possess.

## Compare the task rather than every feature

| Alternative | Why it can feel attractive | Question your offer must answer |
| Existing manual process | Familiar and already working | Why change now? |
| Internal build | Control over requirements | What maintenance effort is avoided? |
| Competing product | Known category and established workflow | What specific situation do you fit better? |
| Doing nothing | No immediate migration effort | What real consequence makes action worthwhile? |

Do not invent a competitor's weakness. Check current capabilities when making a public comparison, and distinguish unavailable information from an actual missing feature. If you cannot substantiate a product comparison, publish your evaluation criteria rather than a false verdict.

## Worked example: reporting software versus a spreadsheet

Illustrative example: a reporting tool competes with a spreadsheet maintained by one employee. Its strongest message may be continuity when that employee is unavailable, not the number of charts. The proof should show another team member producing the required report using the documented workflow.

The spreadsheet can still be the sensible choice for a small team with simple reporting needs. Say who the product is for and where the alternative remains adequate. Clear boundaries help suitable buyers evaluate fit and reduce the risk of selling a larger solution to a smaller problem.

## Convert the map into proof requirements

For each meaningful difference, specify what would demonstrate it: a walkthrough, a sample output, a documented integration or a migration checklist. Avoid moving directly from a desired difference to an unsupported headline such as “zero effort” or “the only tool you need.”

Update the map when customer evidence changes. A competitor introducing a feature may matter less than customers changing how they work. The map should guide sales conversations, landing-page sections and product priorities; it is not an exhaustive market encyclopedia.

## Create your first map

List the last five serious buying decisions you know about and record the actual alternative in each. Choose one recurring tradeoff and build a proof asset that answers it. Use the [switch interview guide](jobs-to-be-done-switch-interview.html) when the decision history is unclear.

@@ marketing-pre-mortem
META: Run a marketing pre-mortem to uncover launch risks, assign owners and define pause conditions with a practical failure-scenario worksheet.
ANSWER: A marketing pre-mortem asks the team to imagine a launch has failed and identify plausible reasons. Turn each reason into a prevention action, detection signal or recovery plan. The exercise is useful only when it changes the launch brief and gives someone responsibility.
RELATED: growth-team-roles,creative-production-capacity,lifecycle-message-qa

## Define failure before imagining it

Choose a concrete outcome and horizon. “The launch was disappointing” invites vague opinions. “The campaign spent its test budget but produced few qualified buyers, while support complaints increased” gives the group something specific to investigate.

Invite people who see different parts of the journey: marketing, product, fulfillment, sales and support. Let them write risks independently before discussion. A person who handles customer complaints may notice a problem the campaign owner does not see.

## Sort risks by where they happen

| Failure scenario | Early warning | Prevention or response |
| Offer overpromises | Repeated questions about unavailable features | Verify claims against the actual product |
| Follow-up is slow | Qualified inquiries wait without an owner | Assign coverage and escalation |
| Tracking is incomplete | Known test purchases are missing | Validate events before scaling |
| Inventory is insufficient | Available stock falls below commitments | Restrict promotion and communicate availability |
| Automation repeats itself | Customers receive duplicate reminders | Test exits, suppression and event handling |

These are example categories, not a universal risk ranking. The highest priority depends on the likelihood, consequence and reversibility of the failure in your business.

## Worked example: a campaign that sells the wrong promise

Illustrative example: an ad says an analytics service is ready immediately, but new customers need a manual data review. The launch team imagines a spike in disappointed buyers and refunds. The fix is not merely another disclaimer at the bottom of the page.

Rewrite the main promise, state the review step before payment and prepare a clear confirmation message. Give support a single owner for stalled setups. Monitor questions about timing as a guardrail. This changes the actual purchase experience rather than protecting an inaccurate headline with fine print.

## Decide which risks block launch

Some issues can be observed safely during a limited test. Others invalidate the offer or measurement immediately. Mark risks as launch blockers, watch items or accepted limitations, and explain why. A lack of reliable stock information may block a sales push; uncertain creative preference may simply be what the test is intended to learn.

Record a pause condition in operational terms. “Pause if customers are upset” is hard to execute. “Pause this promise if the advertised setup cannot be delivered to new buyers” gives the team a direct decision. Keep a rollback path for the experience and its communications.

## Run the meeting in a useful way

Choose one upcoming launch, write a specific failure outcome and collect independent reasons. Leave the meeting with five or fewer prioritized actions, each with an owner and readiness check. Revisit the sheet after launch to compare predicted risks with actual problems.

@@ first-100-customers-strategy
META: Plan your first 100 customers around a narrow segment, direct learning and verified customer outcomes rather than a broad launch or vanity sign-ups.
ANSWER: Getting your first 100 customers is a learning problem before it is a scaling problem. Start with a specific group you can serve, a clear paid offer and a repeatable way to speak with suitable buyers. Track completed customer outcomes, not just registrations or promises to buy.

## Decide what counts as a customer

A free account, an unpaid order and a paying active customer are different milestones. Define the commitment you want to count and the useful outcome that follows it. For a store, you might count delivered paid orders; for software, paid accounts that reach a defined first outcome.

Choose a narrow buying situation. “Everyone who needs marketing” does not help you reach the right person. A recognizable problem, timing trigger and current workaround give you a reason for a conversation and a basis for evaluating fit.

## Start with conversations you can learn from

Paul Graham's essay argues that early founders often need to recruit and help users personally. Treat that as a way to learn, not a prediction that any manual tactic will work. Use relevant introductions, communities where participation is permitted and direct conversations with people facing the problem.

Do not buy an indiscriminate contact list or pretend a mass message is personal research. Explain who you help and what you are asking. Keep refusal easy. A small set of suitable conversations can expose an offer problem more clearly than a large pile of irrelevant traffic.

| Stage | Record | Reason |
| Relevant conversation | Situation and current workaround | Understand whether the need exists |
| Offer presented | Scope, price and next step | Make interest comparable |
| Commitment | Actual payment or defined purchase | Separate intent from action |
| First outcome | Useful result the customer achieves | Check delivery and fit |
| Follow-up | Continued use, issues or repeat order | Learn beyond the first sale |

## Worked example: the target is not 100 sign-ups

Illustrative example: a small software team speaks with 40 suitable businesses. Twelve try a paid pilot, eight complete the intended workflow and five continue after the pilot. The useful review is why four pilots did not reach the outcome and why three activated accounts did not continue.

You cannot infer a reliable market-wide conversion rate from this small, selected group. You can identify specific questions for the next cohort: integration compatibility, pricing, implementation effort or whether the recurring task is valuable enough.

## Make the next ten easier to serve

Record objections, onboarding questions and the work required to deliver. Turn repeated explanations into a demonstration or checklist. Automate only the steps you understand well enough to specify. A repeatable process should preserve the useful conversation rather than erase it.

Set a learning budget and review the cost of founder time. Manual acquisition is not free merely because it uses no ad spend. Once delivery is consistent and the customer pattern is clearer, choose a channel that can reach more people in that same situation.

## Plan the first cohort

Write your customer definition, narrow segment and first useful outcome. Aim to learn from the next ten suitable buyers before designing a campaign for the next thousand. Review the [concierge onboarding guide](onboarding-human-concierge.html) to capture what should eventually become self-service.

@@ growth-model-spreadsheet
META: Build a simple growth model spreadsheet linking traffic, qualified demand, conversion and customer retention, with formulas and sensitivity checks.
ANSWER: A growth model spreadsheet connects business outcomes to a few observable drivers. Keep the inputs, formulas and assumptions separate. Use it to test how a change could affect the business, not to turn uncertain conversion rates into a precise forecast.
RELATED: marketing-budget-allocation,funnel-denominator-audit,ad-spend-cash-flow

## Choose one acquisition path first

A model becomes difficult to audit when it mixes demos, free trials, store orders and renewals in one conversion rate. Start with a single journey and reporting period. Document whether stages refer to people, accounts or orders, and whether outcomes are mature.

For a demo-led business, a simple model might use relevant visits, booking rate, attendance and close rate. Include the time between stages. Customers acquired this month may originate from bookings made earlier, so a monthly activity forecast is not automatically a cohort forecast.

## Keep formulas visible

| Driver | Illustrative input | Calculation |
| Relevant visits | 2,000 | Starting assumption |
| Booking rate | 5% | 2,000 × 0.05 = 100 bookings |
| Attendance | 70% | 100 × 0.70 = 70 attended demos |
| Close rate | 20% | 70 × 0.20 = 14 customers |

This example is a planning scenario, not a portfolio result or benchmark. All rates need consistent populations. If the close rate describes qualified demos but the attendance row includes all demos, add a qualification stage rather than silently multiplying incompatible rates.

## Add service capacity and retention

Estimate what the business can onboard without degrading delivery. If capacity is eight customers in the period, forecasting 14 sales does not create six extra implementation slots. Model the delay, staffing response or limit on acquisition.

For recurring revenue, separate new, retained, expanded and lost accounts. Avoid applying one churn rate to every customer age without checking the underlying behavior. Show when revenue is collected and distinguish it from invoiced or recognized revenue if that distinction matters to the decision.

## Test the assumptions that drive the conclusion

Change one uncertain input at a time, then examine a combined downside case. In the example, reducing attendance from 70% to 50% lowers expected customers from 14 to ten while the other assumptions stay fixed. Reducing both attendance and close rate has a larger effect.

Document why an input might change: seasonality, audience mix, qualification, product readiness or sales coverage. A range based on observed variation is more useful than unexplained “best” and “worst” guesses. Keep forecasts separate from actuals so the team can learn which assumptions were wrong.

## Create the first version

Build tabs for assumptions, calculations and actual outcomes. Add a note beside each rate naming its source, population and window. If an input has no evidence, label it as an assumption. Review the model after outcomes mature and update the driver, not just the final target.

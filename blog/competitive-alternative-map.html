Competitive positioning: map the alternatives your customers actually consider

Build a competitive positioning map around current workarounds, switching costs and buying criteria instead of copying a competitor feature grid.

Ayoub’s Growth Notebook2 min readPrepared 13 October 2026AI-assisted guide
The short answerCompetitive positioning starts with the alternatives customers can choose, including spreadsheets, internal work, another supplier and doing nothing. Map the buying task, benefits, drawbacks and switching effort. A direct competitor list alone can miss the option that actually wins the decision.

Ask what the customer would do instead

Use recent buying conversations to identify alternatives actually considered. Avoid assuming the largest brand in the category is the main rival. A founder may choose to keep a manual process because migration seems riskier than the inconvenience.

Separate direct evidence from your interpretation. “Customer uses spreadsheets today” is observed. “Customer wants a centralized dashboard” needs supporting evidence. An alternative map becomes useful when it explains the tradeoff the buyer recognizes, not when it lists features your product happens to possess.

Compare the task rather than every feature

AlternativeWhy it can feel attractiveQuestion your offer must answer
Existing manual processFamiliar and already workingWhy change now?
Internal buildControl over requirementsWhat maintenance effort is avoided?
Competing productKnown category and established workflowWhat specific situation do you fit better?
Doing nothingNo immediate migration effortWhat real consequence makes action worthwhile?

Do not invent a competitor's weakness. Check current capabilities when making a public comparison, and distinguish unavailable information from an actual missing feature. If you cannot substantiate a product comparison, publish your evaluation criteria rather than a false verdict.

Worked example: reporting software versus a spreadsheet

Illustrative example: a reporting tool competes with a spreadsheet maintained by one employee. Its strongest message may be continuity when that employee is unavailable, not the number of charts. The proof should show another team member producing the required report using the documented workflow.

The spreadsheet can still be the sensible choice for a small team with simple reporting needs. Say who the product is for and where the alternative remains adequate. Clear boundaries help suitable buyers evaluate fit and reduce the risk of selling a larger solution to a smaller problem.

Convert the map into proof requirements

For each meaningful difference, specify what would demonstrate it: a walkthrough, a sample output, a documented integration or a migration checklist. Avoid moving directly from a desired difference to an unsupported headline such as “zero effort” or “the only tool you need.”

Update the map when customer evidence changes. A competitor introducing a feature may matter less than customers changing how they work. The map should guide sales conversations, landing-page sections and product priorities; it is not an exhaustive market encyclopedia.

Create your first map

List the last five serious buying decisions you know about and record the actual alternative in each. Choose one recurring tradeoff and build a proof asset that answers it. Use the switch interview guide when the decision history is unclear.

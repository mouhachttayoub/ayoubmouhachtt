Google Ads search intent: match keywords to the buyer's next step

Group Google Ads searches by buying intent, separate education from evaluation and align the advertisement and landing page with the visitor's next decision.

By Ayoub Mouhachtt3 min read7 October 2026AI-assisted guide
The short answerSearch intent is the task a person is trying to complete with a query. A Google Ads keyword plan becomes more useful when it distinguishes problem research, solution evaluation and action. The landing page should help with that task instead of sending every searcher to the same generic destination.

Read the query as a customer situation

A category query can indicate different needs. Someone searching for a template may want a free file, a paid product or instructions. Someone searching for software may be comparing vendors or solving a specific workflow. The wording is a clue, not a complete description of the person.

Look at actual search terms and the business outcome they produce. Group recurring queries by their task, then compare qualification and conversion lag. Do not judge intent solely by whether the phrase contains “buy” or “price.”

Create a simple intent map

Query situationPage jobOutcome to inspect
Learning about a problemExplain the issue and a suitable next step.Relevant engagement and progression.
Comparing approachesClarify fit, tradeoffs and proof.Qualified evaluation actions.
Looking for a specific solutionShow the matching use case and conditions.Appropriate trial, booking or purchase.
Seeking an existing brandHelp the visitor reach the right destination.The intended business action.

Align the campaign journey

  1. Name the buying question behind each keyword group.
  2. Write an ad that answers that question without overpromising.
  3. Send the visitor to a page that continues the same explanation.
  4. Track a meaningful downstream outcome alongside the initial action.
  5. Review irrelevant searches and qualified customer feedback together.

A high-intent label should not become an excuse to ignore poor fit. A person may urgently want something your offer does not provide.

Worked example: two software searches

Illustrative example: “how to automate order confirmation” and “order confirmation software pricing” may both relate to the same product. The first visitor may need a workflow demonstration; the second may need scope, setup requirements and pricing conditions. Separate page sections or destinations can make the next decision clearer.

If both queries go to a vague homepage, the team may interpret weak conversion as low demand when the real issue is missing information.

Measure the whole buying step

Compare query groups using qualified outcomes, not only click-through rate. A clear ad may receive fewer clicks because it helps poor-fit searchers exclude themselves. That can be valuable when sales time or fulfillment capacity is limited.

Is informational intent always unsuitable for ads?

No, but the economics and next step differ. Educational traffic may require a longer path and should not be evaluated as if every click is ready to purchase.

Can you identify intent perfectly from keywords?

No. Use queries, page behavior and customer outcomes together. Treat the initial grouping as a hypothesis to improve.

Put this into practice

Select ten recent search terms, write the task behind each and check whether the destination page answers it. Repair the most obvious mismatch before expanding the keyword list.
